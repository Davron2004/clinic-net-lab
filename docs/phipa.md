# Network design and PHIPA

This page maps each design choice in the lab to the part of Ontario's *Personal Health Information Protection Act, 2004* (PHIPA), its general regulation (O. Reg. 329/04), or guidance from the Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario (IPC) that it supports. A small clinic run by physicians is a "health information custodian" (s. 3(1), para. 1: "a health care practitioner or a person who operates a group practice of health care practitioners"), and its staff are its "agents" (s. 2). PHIPA does not mention networks: its core security duty (s. 12(1)) is to take "steps that are reasonable in the circumstances", and the IPC has said that this duty "does not call for perfection" and that "there is no detailed prescription in PHIPA for what is reasonable" (PHIPA Decision 253, para. 54). So the choices below are **evidence of reasonable technical safeguards**, not proof that a clinic complies with PHIPA.

*Not legal advice. Citations checked against the official texts on 2026-10-26.*

| Design choice | What it does in the lab | PHIPA / regulation / IPC reference | Why it supports that obligation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zones and default deny.** Staff, servers, medical devices, printers/fax and guest Wi-Fi each get their own VLAN. Traffic between zones is dropped unless a rule allows it, and each allow rule covers one source, one destination and one service. | `config/clinic/switch/ports.conf` (VLANs 10–50), `config/clinic/router/nftables.nft` (`policy drop`, one `from_<zone>` chain per zone). Allowed paths: A01–A10. | [s. 12(1)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19): protect PHI "against theft, loss and unauthorized use or disclosure". IPC ransomware fact sheet: "Segregate IT assets into different network security zones… Network activity between these zones could then be monitored or limited." | A compromised guest phone, printer or device can't reach the servers that hold PHI. This is the measure the IPC names for limiting an attacker's movement through the network. |
| **Staff use records only through the EMR app.** Staff PCs can't open the raw records file share and can't get an admin shell on the EMR server. | A01, A02 allowed (HTTPS to the EMR). D17 (SMB to the EMR) and D18 (SSH to the EMR) denied. | [s. 17(2)–(3)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK26): an agent may use PHI only where "necessary for the purpose of carrying out his or her duties", and the custodian must take reasonable steps to ensure this. IPC fact sheet: follow "the 'principle of least privilege'" and "minimize and monitor the use of administrator accounts". IPC *Detecting and Deterring*: restrict access "on a need-to-know basis". | The EMR app is where each user's role, each patient record and the access log are enforced. A path around the app would also get around those controls. Note the limit: the network can't tell *which patient* a user opens. Only the EMR can enforce and log that. |
| **Medical devices do exactly one job.** The imaging workstation may only send DICOM to the image archive, and the lab analyzer may only send HL7 to the EMR. Devices get no internet, no DNS, and no path to each other. | A05, A06 allowed. D07–D13 and D23 denied. Rules `deny:device-internet`, `deny:devices`. Switch ports eth6/eth7 are `isolated`. | [s. 12(1)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19). IPC fact sheet: hardening means "reducing the number of pathways that an attacker can take", and organizations should "develop strategies to mitigate risk to systems that are out of date". | Imaging and lab devices often run old, unpatchable software. Since they can't be fixed, the network limits what an infected device can reach. |
| **No internet for the EMR server or printers.** | D16 (EMR to the internet), D14 and D15 (printer to the internet and to staff PCs) denied. Rules `deny:server-internet`, `deny:printer-internet`. | [s. 12(1)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19) (theft, unauthorized disclosure). IPC fact sheet: data removal during an attack "would generally be considered a theft". It also recommends that organizations "block network traffic of irregular file transfers to untrusted destinations". | The server that holds the records has no direct route out, so an attacker can't upload (exfiltrate) records from it straight to the internet. |
| **Guest Wi-Fi is treated as the internet.** Guests get web access and DNS only, and guest devices can't reach each other. | A08, A09 allowed. D01–D06 denied. Switch ports eth9/eth10 are `isolated`. | [s. 12(1)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19). | Patients' and visitors' devices are untrusted. Keeping them off the clinic network removes the most direct route to the EMR, the image archive and staff PCs. |
| **No inbound access except the VPN** *(remote access: in progress)*. A doctor at home connects over WireGuard and reaches only the EMR web app. Keys are generated on the device. | D20–D22 denied (no inbound connections, no EMR access without the VPN). R-series flows are being added. | [s. 12(1)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19). IPC fact sheet: attackers who exploit internet-facing systems "commonly target an organization's infrastructure that supports remote access". It recommends "strong multi-factor authentication". | Only one port is exposed to the internet, and a remote user gets one service, not the whole network. Gap: a WireGuard key is a device credential, not multi-factor authentication. |
| **Every allowed new connection and every blocked attempt is logged with the rule's name.** | `log group 0 prefix "allow:<rule>"` / `"deny:<rule>"` in `nftables.nft`, written to `run/logs/firewall.jsonl`. | [s. 12(2)–(3)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19) and [O. Reg. 329/04, s. 6.3](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/040329#BK7): the custodian must notify individuals, and in prescribed cases the Commissioner, of theft, loss or unauthorized use or disclosure. IPC fact sheet: keep "procedures for analyzing network logs to determine if data removal (exfiltration) has occurred". | Whether notice or reporting is required depends on what happened, for example whether data was stolen (s. 6.3(1), para. 2). The network log is the evidence for that. See the note on s. 10.1 below. |
| **Monitoring** *(in progress)*. Wazuh SIEM raises alerts when a medical device tries the internet or a host scans the network. File-integrity monitoring watches the patient records. | S-series flows. `run/alerts.jsonl`. | [s. 12(1)–(2)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19) (notice "at the first reasonable opportunity"). IPC fact sheet: "Develop a baseline… identify irregular traffic", "Monitor the integrity of records for irregular changes to large numbers of files", "Consider using a security information and event management solution". | A breach that nobody notices can't be contained or reported on time. These three IPC recommendations map one-to-one onto the alerts. |
| **A written policy tested by real traffic in CI.** The expected flows are written down separately from the firewall. A test suite sends real packets for each one and writes a report. | `policy/clinic.yml` (33 flows), `tests/isolation.py` (writes `run/results/report.md` with a ruleset fingerprint), `.github/workflows/ci.yml` (runs on every change). On the flat "before" network it caught 19 violations. | [s. 10(1)–(2)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK14): have information practices and "comply with its information practices". [s. 2](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK3) defines these to include "the administrative, technical and physical safeguards". IPC fact sheet: "regular monitoring, testing, or auditing of those practices". | `clinic.yml` is the written technical safeguard. The suite checks on every change that the network still does what that policy says. |
| **Ransomware simulation.** Ransomware starts on the imaging workstation and tries every path an attacker would. | `scenarios/ransomware.py`. On the flat network it encrypted 12 of 12 synthetic records. On the segmented network it encrypted 0. | [s. 12(1)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK19) (records protected against "unauthorized copying, modification or disposal") and [s. 13(1)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/04p03#BK21) (records "retained… in a secure manner"). [PHIPA Decision 253](https://www.ipc.on.ca/en/decisions/latest-decisions/phipa-decision-253): ransomware encryption was "both an unauthorized use and a loss" under s. 12(2), even without proof that anyone viewed the data. | The flat result is a notifiable breach under the IPC's reading of s. 12(2). The segmented result shows that the zone rules prevented that specific harm. |

**On s. 10.1 (electronic audit logs).** Section 10.1 would require custodians that use electronic means to keep an audit log of every time a PHI record is "viewed, handled, modified or otherwise dealt with". The log would record the type of information, the date and time, who did it, and whose information it was. e-Laws lists it as **not in force** (2020, c. 5, Sched. 6, s. 3). Even once it is in force, a firewall log can't meet it, because the firewall sees IP addresses and ports, not users or patients. That log belongs in the EMR. The IPC's *Detecting and Deterring* paper already recommends EMR logs with that content.

## What this lab does not cover

- Consent, the purposes for which PHI is collected, used and disclosed, and the public statement of information practices (s. 16).
- Patients' requests for access and correction, and the contact person's duties (s. 15).
- Controls inside the EMR: which role can see which record, the per-patient audit log, privacy warning flags.
- Staff privacy training, confidentiality agreements and discipline.
- Physical security, device encryption, backups and disaster recovery, retention schedules, and secure disposal (s. 13(1)).
- Multi-factor authentication, patching and endpoint protection.
- Agreements with IT and EMR vendors (s. 10(4); O. Reg. 329/04, s. 6).
- Breach response itself: notifying individuals and the IPC (s. 12(2)–(3)), the annual breach statistics report to the IPC (O. Reg. 329/04, s. 6.4), and a written incident response plan.
